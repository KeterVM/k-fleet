# Prototype and evaluate

## Match fidelity to the question

Identify what the prototype must let someone perceive or do. Paper, a flow diagram,
clickable screens, and working code answer different questions. Choose the cheapest
form that represents the disputed behavior faithfully. Use realistic content and
constraints; mark simulated data, integrations, and outcomes so they are not mistaken
for implemented capabilities.

Follow existing visual and component conventions unless the design task calls them
into question. Make intentional choices about hierarchy and emphasis. Aesthetic
consistency matters, but it cannot replace task completion, understandable content,
or accessible operation.

## Use observations to change the design

For authorized usability work, choose tasks tied to real goals and participants
whose relevant conditions match intended use. Avoid instructions that reveal the
control or path being evaluated. Observe where people hesitate, fail, recover, or
misunderstand the outcome. Separate observed actions from stated preferences and
the evaluator's explanation.

Account for prototype limitations before attributing a failure to the concept.
Likewise, do not excuse a real interaction problem merely because the backend is
simulated. Prioritize issues by their effect on the task and affected users, including
access needs, rather than frequency alone in a small sample.

When users are unavailable, perform a bounded task walkthrough and relevant
accessibility or platform checks; label this as inspection, not user validation.
Never manufacture research participants or simulated approval. Stop once the
specific design uncertainty is resolved or cannot be answered with available evidence.

Carry the supported changes and remaining uncertainty into implementation. When
runtime behavior matters, check the implemented interaction on the relevant surface;
prototype evidence does not prove that the delivered controls, states, or focus
behavior match the design.
