# Interpretation and decisions

## Explain patterns without overclaiming

Compare relevant populations, periods, and workflow stages. Check exposure to the
change, seasonality, acquisition shifts, simultaneous releases, and measurement
changes before attributing an effect. Report absolute counts with rates where small
denominators could mislead. Averages can hide a failing group or an important tail.

Use feedback to investigate why behavior occurs, while accounting for who supplied
it and who is absent. A frequent complaint in support cases is not its prevalence
among all users. Separate observations from causal explanations and state credible
alternatives that could change the proposed action.

## Choose a defensible experiment

When experimentation is authorized and useful, define the hypothesis, assignment
unit, exposure, primary outcome, relevant adverse effects, and decision conditions
before starting. Account for available sample, baseline variability, outcome delay,
interference between participants, and material implementation constraints. Use
appropriate statistical guidance or specialist support for consequential inference;
do not invent significance, power, or a universal sample size.

Check assignment and measurement integrity before interpreting a difference. Avoid
declaring success by repeatedly looking for a favorable result or selecting the
winning metric after the fact. Safety stopping conditions and statistical stopping
rules serve different purposes; use a suitable design if repeated decisions are
needed.

Where randomization is infeasible, use a proportionate alternative such as observed
task performance, a bounded pilot, or a carefully qualified comparison. State the
causal limits. Small samples can reveal usability failures without establishing a
population-wide lift.

## Turn evidence into a next action

Connect the recommendation to the problem and its likely cause. A broken flow may
need an implementation fix; a confusing interaction may need experience design;
weak value evidence may require returning to discovery. Do not treat every weak
metric as a request for more features or a change to the user's goals.

State the tradeoff, uncertainty, and observation needed to judge the next change.
Stop the investigation when more analysis cannot change the decision or requires
unavailable evidence. Preserve a concise decision record where future work needs
it, without making a dashboard, experiment report, or formal document mandatory.
