# Outcomes and scope

## Understand successful use

Find who needs the change, in what situation, and what successful use enables.
Distinguish the requester, the actor, and other affected people or systems when
their needs or permissions differ. Use established domain terms; clarify ambiguous
words that could change behavior rather than inventing a broad glossary.

Trace a representative scenario: its trigger, starting conditions, action, observable
result, and what happens next. Include relevant recipients and alternate entry points
such as batch or background work. Check that the intended outcome remains achievable
across the whole scenario, including necessary handoffs or setup outside the immediate
screen or endpoint.

Ground the scenario in the request and project evidence. Label illustrative examples
as proposals until supported; a convincing story is not evidence of user intent.
Reuse relevant existing research or observed behavior when available, without claiming
that a newly invented persona represents actual users.

## Set boundaries that preserve the goal

Separate essential behavior, optional improvements, explicit exclusions, and deferred
decisions using the user's priorities and established constraints. Explain a material
boundary through what it includes or excludes from successful use. Do not relabel a
required part as optional merely because it is expensive or inconvenient.

For a proposed smaller first delivery, check whether the user can still achieve the
promised outcome. If it changes recipients, reach, timeliness, or required manual work,
present that consequence as a product choice rather than a routine implementation
shortcut. Distinguish work sequencing from a reduction in the requested scope.

Record why consequential requirements are needed and where they come from in the
task or project context. Keep these connections lightweight; a formal traceability
matrix is unnecessary unless the project requires it. Keep unsupported additions out
of committed scope and identify essential steps omitted by a narrow feature description.

Preserve explicit solution constraints. Where the user has left the mechanism open,
describe required behavior before choosing schemas, queues, frameworks, or topology.
A requirement may constrain implementation for a real reason; make that reason clear
rather than treating all solution detail as either mandatory or forbidden.
