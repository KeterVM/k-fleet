# Discover product

Establish which user problem is worth addressing and what evidence supports the
next product decision. Use for uncertain opportunities or requested discovery;
reuse adequate research when the problem and commitment are already established.
A clear feature request does not automatically require market research.

## Select supporting guidance

- For users, existing alternatives, problem severity, or competing opportunities,
  read [Problem evidence](references/problem-evidence.md).
- For an untested value proposition or a decision about further investment, read
  [Validate the opportunity](references/validate-the-opportunity.md).

## Scope and authority

Preserve the user's goals, audience, constraints, and accepted commitments. Research
may challenge an assumption; it does not authorize changing the product or dropping
required scope. Distinguish discovering a valuable problem from defining the
behavior of an agreed solution, which belongs to `kf-define-requirements`.

Use current sources within the authorized project and data scope. Separate observed
behavior, participant statements, external claims, and inference. Record enough
provenance and context to judge whether evidence applies. Do not invent interviews,
market figures, customer quotes, willingness to pay, or validation results.

Investigate answerable facts directly. Ask for unresolved user intent when different
answers change the audience, goal, scope, or commitment, and wait before dependent
work. Continue independent research. Research authorization does not by itself
authorize contacting people, publishing experiments, spending money, or collecting
additional personal data; reuse any explicit authorization already present.

## Resolve the next product decision

Identify the decision at stake and the assumption whose failure would most change
it. Understand what people currently do, what outcome they seek, where the current
approach fails, and why changing behavior could be worthwhile. Consider existing
alternatives, including doing nothing, before proposing new features.

Choose evidence suited to the uncertainty. Observation can expose workflow problems;
interviews can explain context; a prototype can test comprehension; actual adoption
or a bounded commercial experiment may bear on demand. Do not treat these as
interchangeable proof. Match the effort to the consequence of a wrong decision.

Compare opportunities using the user's priorities, problem evidence, feasibility,
and material delivery or operating constraints. Avoid invented numerical scores or
market precision. Preserve conflicting evidence rather than averaging away distinct
user groups. Use specialist methods when necessary without making them prerequisites
for every discovery task.

Define what result would support, weaken, or leave the hypothesis unresolved before
running a probe. AI-generated personas and simulated feedback can suggest questions;
they cannot establish real user needs or demand.

## Finish with a bounded conclusion

Carry forward the supported problem, audience, evidence, remaining hypotheses, and
the recommended next decision: proceed, narrow, investigate, defer, or stop. Keep
recommendations distinct from approved commitments. Use a short brief or existing
project record when useful; do not require a document template.

Stop when evidence is sufficient for the requested decision, or when additional
progress depends on unavailable access, participants, or user choices. State the
consequence of missing evidence and the observation that would change the conclusion.
If execution is also authorized, continue the agreed work; discovery is not an
automatic approval gate.
