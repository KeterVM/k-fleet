# Problem evidence

## Understand the current behavior

Identify the affected people through their tasks and conditions, not an invented
demographic persona. Trace a recent concrete attempt: trigger, intended outcome,
current tools or workarounds, friction, consequences, and who makes or pays for the
decision. Users, buyers, administrators, and support staff may have different needs.

Use available observations, support cases, research, and authorized product data.
Check where evidence came from, how recent it is, and which users or situations are
missing. A repeated complaint can justify investigation without establishing its
frequency across the whole audience. Competitor features demonstrate an available
alternative, not demand for copying it.

When interviews are authorized, ask about actual events and current behavior before
proposing a solution. Avoid leading questions and treating politeness or stated
interest as commitment. Retain relevant contradictions and separate quotes from
interpretation. Minimize personal data in notes and follow the project's handling
requirements.

## Judge whether the opportunity fits

Describe the change in outcome the product might enable and the cost of switching
from the current approach. Consider the consequences of leaving the problem unsolved,
its recurrence, available workarounds, and the user's strategic or resource limits.
Commercial products may need evidence about purchasing and distribution; internal
tools may instead depend on workflow adoption and time saved. Do not impose a
revenue model on every product.

Distinguish an evidenced problem, a proposed solution, and an assumption about its
value. Missing demand evidence calls for a proportionate probe, not a larger feature
list. If evidence points to a different audience or goal, present the tradeoff for
the user's decision before redirecting dependent delivery.
